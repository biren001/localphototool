What this compressor actually does

Most online compressors work the same way: your file is uploaded, passed through a fixed preset such as "quality 80", and sent back. That preset has no idea what your image contains, so a smooth sky and a photo of tree branches receive identical treatment — one ends up unnecessarily large, the other unnecessarily degraded.

This tool inverts the process. Your image is analysed first, then encoded to fit the evidence. It is the same strategy used by professional build pipelines, running in a browser tab you can close at any time — and entirely on your own hardware, so nothing is uploaded. If you want to check that rather than believe it, the compress without uploading page walks through a ten-second test you can run on any tool, including this one.

The five things that make the output smaller

  1. Measured quality instead of a fixed preset. A small proxy is encoded at several quality levels and decoded again; each result is scored against the original using PSNR. The engine keeps the lowest quality that still clears a 40 dB fidelity floor, with headroom so the full-resolution encode stays there too.
  2. Quality-aware format selection. Opaque photographs stay in a photographic codec; graphics with flat colour and few unique tones stay in PNG, where lossless and palette encoding win outright. Across our own measurements AVIF lands about a quarter below MozJPEG at equal measured fidelity, while WebP comes out roughly level with it.
  3. Palette quantization for PNG. Screenshots, diagrams and flat illustrations rarely need 16.7 million colours. A median-cut quantizer reduces them to 256 (or fewer) with optional Floyd–Steinberg dithering, then writes a true indexed PNG — typically 70–90% smaller with no visible banding.
  4. Iterative halving when downscaling. Shrinking a 6000 px photo to 1600 px in one step aliases badly. Halving repeatedly and smoothing at each stage preserves edge structure, which in turn lets the encoder spend fewer bits on the same perceived quality.
  5. Metadata removal. EXIF blocks, thumbnails embedded inside the file, GPS traces and maker notes can add tens of kilobytes on their own. They are dropped, while the orientation tag is applied to the pixels first so nothing ends up sideways.

At equal fidelity, is WebP really smaller than JPEG?

The usual figure is that WebP beats JPEG by 25–50%. We measured it rather than repeating it, by holding fidelity constant at 40 dB — the level this tool's own floor targets — and comparing the bytes each codec needed to get there. The result is more interesting than the slogan, and less flattering to WebP.

Two things explain the gap between this and the familiar claim. The first is the encoder: the 25–50% figure comes from comparing WebP against a baseline JPEG encoder, and MozJPEG — which is what this tool uses — is considerably stronger at high quality settings. The second is the metric: WebP's advantage is largest in perceptual measures such as SSIM and at lower quality settings, while this comparison is in PSNR, which is the definition our own fidelity floor uses. Neither of those makes WebP a bad format, and it remains the safest choice for compatibility. It does mean that AVIF is the one to pick when the smallest possible file is the goal, and that the honest headline for photographs is AVIF, not WebP.

How these numbers were produced

  • Corpus. Three 2400×1600 photographs, each in three states: as an already-optimised JPEG, re-encoded at Q96 to stand in for a straight-from-camera file, and stored losslessly as a 3 MB PNG. Plus one 1440×900 interface screenshot generated in the browser so it is identical on every run. All files are pinned by SHA-256 and the measurement script prints every hash it used, so a changed encoder shows up as a changed hash rather than as a quiet shift in the table.
  • Fidelity. PSNR on luma, measured the same way the engine's own quality search measures it, against the original pixels at full resolution.
  • Encoders. MozJPEG (WebAssembly) for JPEG, the @jsquash builds for WebP and AVIF, and the browser's own canvas encoder plus this site's median-cut quantiser for PNG. A case that silently fell back to a browser-native encoder is never published as a WebAssembly result.
  • Timing. Every case is encoded twice. The first pass pays for the codec download and WebAssembly instantiation, and only the second pass is reported — a cold-start number would misdescribe normal use.
  • Environment. Chromium headless, Windows Server, Node 22. Output size depends only on the encoders and should hold anywhere; the timings depend on the machine and should be read as indicative.
  • Reproduce. node _dev/measure-compression-matrix.cjs produces the first table, node _dev/measure-format-efficiency.cjs the second. Both drive the real engine in this page.

Known variance: AVIF is by far the slowest encoder here and its quality search can hit its patience budget on a loaded machine, so AVIF figures — especially the savings on already-optimised photos, which ranged from 0% to 37% across runs — are the least stable in the table. Measured 2026-09-20.

Frequently asked questions

How do I compress an image to exactly 200 KB?

Set Strategy to Target size and type 200 in the KB field. The engine binary-searches the encoder quality across up to seven passes, and if even the lowest sensible quality overshoots, it downscales the image step by step until the file fits. The result panel shows exactly what it did.

Does compressing reduce image quality?

Lossy formats always discard something, but Auto mode holds measured fidelity above ~40 dB PSNR, which is past the point where differences are visible at normal viewing sizes. If you want certainty rather than heuristics, use Quality mode with a high setting or export PNG, which is lossless.

Can I compress a whole folder at once?

Select as many files as you like in the file picker, or drag a multi-selection straight onto the page. They process in parallel background workers, and Download all packages every finished file into a single ZIP — duplicate names are de-duplicated automatically.

Why did one of my files come back unchanged?

If re-encoding would have produced a larger file, the original is kept instead and the row is labelled "already optimal". This happens with images that were already compressed aggressively — there is genuinely nothing left to remove, and shipping a bigger file would be a downgrade.

Is my data safe?

Your files are only ever read by your own browser. There is no server-side component that accepts image data. The only network requests the page makes are for the site's own assets and, optionally, the WASM codec modules from a public CDN — never your images.

How do I compress an image to under 100 KB?

Pick Target size, enter 100 and run it. The engine searches for the highest quality that still lands under 100 KB, and only reduces the pixel dimensions if no quality setting can reach the budget — so you keep the resolution whenever the format allows it. It is the same control for 50 KB, 200 KB or 2 MB.

What is the difference between WebP and AVIF?

We measured this rather than repeating the usual figure. Encoding three 2400×1600 photographs to reach the same 40 dB, AVIF needed about 26% fewer bytes than MozJPEG, while WebP came out roughly level with JPEG — a little smaller on two of the three images, and noticeably larger on a highly detailed one. That surprises people, because WebP is usually quoted as 25–50% ahead of JPEG: the gap is real in perceptual metrics and at lower quality settings, but this tool's fidelity floor is defined in PSNR, and MozJPEG is unusually strong there. AVIF is the pick when size is the priority; both keep transparency, and AVIF costs noticeably more encoding time. WebP is supported by every current browser and by most CMSs; AVIF support is now broad but still patchy in older editors and some upload forms.

Why did my PNG barely get smaller?

Usually one of three reasons. The file was already optimised, so there is very little redundancy left to remove. Or it is a photograph saved as PNG, where lossless encoding is the wrong tool — exporting our 3 MB test photograph as WebP or JPEG cut 90% and 87% respectively, which the measured table shows in full. Or it uses the full 16.7 million colours, in which case palette quantization has little to work with; lowering the colour count or switching format is what actually helps.

Does it work offline?

Yes. Once the page has loaded, compression runs entirely on your machine. Blocking the CDN only means the optional WASM codecs are unavailable; the built-in browser encoders take over automatically and everything else is identical.
